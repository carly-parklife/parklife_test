The Birthplace of Dallas Parks

City Park, also known as “Old City Park,” holds a unique place in Dallas history as the city’s very first municipal park. Established in 1876, the 22.1-acre park laid the foundation for what would become one of the largest municipal park systems in the country. Over nearly 150 years, City Park has served many roles, from providing the city’s first public water supply and hosting concerts and family gatherings to preserving Dallas’ architectural heritage. Today, it remains one of the city’s most historically significant green spaces, where visitors can explore beautifully preserved historic structures while experiencing the origins of Dallas’ park system.

The park’s mature landscape further connects visitors to Dallas’ past. Native cedar elms, live oaks, bur oaks, and pecan trees shade the walkways and frame the historic buildings, creating a verdant setting that echoes the natural character of North Texas during the era when many of the structures were built. Together, the architecture and mature tree canopy help create a sense of stepping back into the landscape of pioneer Dallas.

Dallas’ First Public Park

In 1876, when Dallas was home to just about 10,000 residents, local businessman J.J. Eakins sold ten acres of land to the City of Dallas for $600. Located around the city’s former Pest House, a small, isolated facility used during the 1870s to quarantine smallpox patients, the property became Dallas’ first public park.

The purchase reflected a growing belief that expanding cities should provide shared public spaces where residents could enjoy nature and gather as a community. As Dallas continued to grow, so did City Park. In 1881, the city acquired an additional eight acres from the Browder family, adding Browder Springs and Mill Creek to the property. These natural springs became Dallas’ first public water supply, making the park essential not only for recreation but also for the city’s early infrastructure.

Community pride in the park grew quickly. In 1882, Dallas citizens formally petitioned city leaders to improve and beautify the grounds. By 1887, the Texas Electric Light and Manufacturing Company installed five electric lights throughout the park, making it one of the city’s earliest illuminated public spaces. That same year, some residents even proposed renaming the park “Eakins Park” in honor of its original landowner, though the name ultimately remained City Park.

The park’s native trees are also part of the landscape story that has developed over generations. Mature cedar elms, live oaks, bur oaks, and pecans now line the walkways and provide shade throughout the grounds, reflecting tree species that have long been characteristic of North Texas. Their presence reinforces the connection between the park’s natural environment and the region’s early history.

From Scenic Retreat to Recreation Destination

Like many parks of the late nineteenth century, City Park was originally designed as a place for quiet reflection and scenic enjoyment. Inspired by the City Beautiful movement and the landscape principles of Frederick Law Olmsted, parks during this era emphasized formal gardens, winding carriage drives, and carefully designed natural landscapes.

City Park’s mature canopy contributes to this enduring park-like character. Large cedar elms, live oaks, bur oaks, and pecans line the walkways and create shaded spaces for visitors, while framing views of the historic buildings throughout the grounds. The trees provide more than visual appeal. Their scale and species evoke the native woodland environment that would have surrounded early public and residential buildings in Dallas, helping the park’s landscape complement its historic architecture.

By 1888, City Park began reflecting changing ideas about recreation. Rather than serving only as an attractive landscape, the park evolved into an active community destination. Public concerts became regular attractions, drawing families from across Dallas. In 1889, the city purchased two sets of American Patent Swings, providing one of the park system’s earliest playground features.

Throughout the 1890s, visitors could also enjoy Dallas’ first public zoo, which featured deer, bears, mountain lions, eagles, rabbits, and other animals before the collection eventually moved first to Fair Park and later to Marsalis Park.

The park became a popular destination for flower shows, family picnics, civic celebrations, and annual meetings of the Dallas County Pioneer Association. During an era when many Americans worked six days a week with little leisure time, City Park offered one of the few places where residents could gather outdoors to enjoy music, nature, and community life.

Preserving Dallas’ Historic Landscape

As Dallas expanded throughout the twentieth century, City Park’s surrounding neighborhood changed dramatically. Following World War II, suburban development and the construction of Interstate 30 contributed to the decline of the once-prominent Cedars neighborhood. The park itself also experienced years of reduced use.

A new chapter began in 1967 when the Dallas County Heritage Society rescued the historic Millermore Mansion from demolition by relocating it to City Park. Recognizing the park as an ideal place to preserve North Texas history, the organization soon began relocating other endangered buildings from across the region to the site.

Over time, the park became home to an extraordinary collection of more than 30 restored nineteenth-century structures, including pioneer log cabins dating to the 1840s, a general store, a country church, a schoolhouse, a train depot, and Victorian homes. Together, these preserved buildings provide a remarkable window into everyday life in early North Texas and reflect the city’s commitment to preserving its architectural and cultural heritage.

The surrounding landscape strengthens that historical experience. Mature native cedar elms, live oaks, bur oaks, and pecans provide a canopy around structures such as the Millermore Mansion, general store, train depot, and old church. These trees echo the landscape character of North Texas during the same period represented by the buildings, creating a historically appropriate setting in which architecture and native woodland exist side by side.

Rather than functioning simply as a backdrop, the mature tree canopy helps reinforce the authenticity of City Park’s historical narrative. The relationship between the preserved buildings and native landscape gives visitors a sense of how early Dallas may have looked and felt, connecting the city’s architectural heritage with the natural environment that shaped the region.

A Park That Connects Past and Present

City Park has witnessed nearly every stage of Dallas’ development. It has served as the city’s first municipal park, protected its earliest water supply, welcomed generations of families, introduced Dallas’ first zoo, and safeguarded some of North Texas’ most significant historic structures.

The park’s history also reflects changing public values. In 1897, a Confederate memorial was installed within City Park as part of a broader movement across the South to commemorate the Civil War. The monument remained for decades before being relocated to Pioneer Park Cemetery in 1961 and ultimately removed in 2020 following court decisions and community discussions about its historical context.

Today, City Park continues to preserve the story of Dallas’ earliest years while providing a peaceful setting for visitors to explore the city’s past. Its mature native trees, historic buildings, shaded walkways, and preserved landscapes work together to create a distinctive sense of place. The oaks, elms, and pecans connect the park’s present-day landscape to the North Texas environment of generations past, while the historic structures tell the story of the people who lived, worked, gathered, and built a growing Dallas.

Nearly 150 years after its founding, City Park remains much more than Dallas’ oldest park. It is a living reminder of how public parks have evolved alongside the city itself, serving recreation, education, historic preservation, and community for generations, while its landscape continues to connect visitors with the natural and cultural history of early Dallas.
