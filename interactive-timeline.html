Founding Era

The Birth of Dallas Parks. On July 4, 1876, the city purchased ten acres of land surrounding Browder Springs, creating City Park, Dallas’ first public park. The founding of City Park marked the beginning of a massive park system featuring hundreds of public parks spread amongst thousands of acres of land.

As Dallas grew, City Park became a place where residents gathered for picnics, concerts, celebrations, and leisure. Over the following decade, the city expanded the park to nearly nineteen acres, laying the foundation for what would eventually become one of the nation’s largest municipal park systems.

For nearly three decades, City Park stood alone as Dallas’ only public park. Yet its success demonstrated the importance of preserving open spaces as the city rapidly expanded.

More Than a Park. City Park reflected Dallas’ growing ambitions. By the mid-1880s, the park featured paved walkways, bridges, landscaped flower beds, newly planted trees, and electric arc lights—modern improvements that made it one of the city’s most attractive public destinations.

In 1888, the park purchased two deer and two mountain lions, creating Dallas’ first public zoo. A bandstand soon hosted musical performances, while greenhouses supported beautiful floral displays throughout the grounds. These additions transformed City Park into a destination for recreation, education, and community life, demonstrating that parks could enrich the city’s culture as well as its landscape.

Today, the site is known as Old City Park, now home to Dallas Heritage Village, preserving the city’s early history while honoring the birthplace of Dallas’ park system.

Preparing for a Park System. As Dallas entered the twentieth century, city leaders recognized that one park could no longer serve a rapidly growing population. In 1904, Dallas acquired Fair Park from the State Fair Association, marking only the second major public park in the city and signaling a new era of expansion.

The following year, the city established the Dallas Park Board, creating a formal organization responsible for planning, managing, and growing Dallas’ parks. Under the leadership of its inaugural board members—including first board president T. Barry Bryan, along with M. N. Baker, J. J. Ecford, Emil Fretz, W. O. O’Connor, Curtis P. Smith, Ben Irelson, S. J. Hay, Murrell Buckner, and Edgar L. Pike—the foundation was laid for a coordinated park system that would transform Dallas over the next century.

What began as a ten-acre tract around a water spring, became the starting point for a citywide vision: creating parks that improved public health, provided recreation, beautified neighborhoods, and ensured that future generations would always have places to gather, play, and connect with nature.

1910–1920
CITY BEAUTIFUL MOVEMENT ERA

City Beautiful Movement Era

A Vision for a More Beautiful Dallas. As Dallas entered the early twentieth century, the city experienced rapid growth driven by industry, commerce, and a rising population. Civic leaders recognized that parks could serve a greater purpose than simply preserving open space. They could help shape a healthier, more beautiful, and more connected city. Inspired by the national City Beautiful Movement, the Dallas Park Board expanded the park system while embracing thoughtful urban planning, grand public spaces, and neighborhood recreation.

Landscape architect George E. Kessler’s 1910 plan transformed this vision into reality. His plan for an interconnected network of parks, parkways, and tree-lined boulevards became Dallas’ first comprehensive parks system, linking neighborhoods with scenic green spaces. The plan also encouraged improvements to waterways, including the Trinity River and Turtle Creek, blending natural landscapes with civic infrastructure. Many of Kessler’s ideas continue to influence Dallas’ layout today.

Parks Become Centers of Community Life. During this era, Dallas parks evolved beyond places of passive enjoyment into active community gathering spaces. While earlier parks emphasized formal gardens and picturesque scenery, the Park Board increasingly invested in recreational facilities and organized programming for residents.

One of the most significant additions was Trinity Play Park, which opened in 1910. More than just a playground, it offered supervised activities and an after-school program for children whose parents worked long hours in nearby cotton mills. This reflected a growing belief that parks could improve public health, provide safe recreation, and support working families.

Other neighborhood parks expanded community access to education and recreation. Turner Plaza became home to Oak Cliff’s first free public library, while Hall Street Negro Park—known today as Griggs Park—was established as a segregated park serving Dallas’ people of color. Despite the inequalities of segregation, the park provided valuable amenities, including baseball fields and swimming facilities, giving residents opportunities for recreation that were often unavailable elsewhere.

A Lasting Legacy. The City Beautiful Movement left a lasting imprint on Dallas. Throughout the 1910s and 1920s, the Park Board significantly expanded the city’s park acreage and strengthened its commitment to serving neighborhoods across Dallas. During this period, civic leaders, including W. M. Holland, Henry D. Lindsley, Joe E. Lawther, Frank W. Wozencraft, Sawnie R. Aldredge, L. Blaylock, R. E. Burt, and J. Waddy Tate, helped guide the park system through a period of remarkable growth. In 1929, Mrs. Laura Sampson Smith became the first woman appointed to the Park Board and contributed the designs for the iconic White Rock Lake stone tables and pavilion.

By combining thoughtful planning with expanded recreation and neighborhood services, this era laid the foundation for the modern Dallas park system. Its influence can still be seen today in Dallas’ connected parkways, civic landscapes, and enduring belief that parks should enrich both the beauty of the city and the lives of its people.

WPA Parks Era

A New Deal for Dallas Parks. Although Dallas weathered the Great Depression better than many American cities thanks in part to the East Texas oil boom, thousands of residents still faced unemployment and economic hardship. As part of President Franklin D. Roosevelt’s New Deal, federal relief programs transformed both the local economy and Dallas’ growing park system. Between 1933 and 1940, agencies such as the Works Progress Administration (WPA), Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC), Public Works Administration (PWA), and National Youth Administration (NYA) invested hundreds of thousands of dollars into parks across the city, creating jobs while leaving behind lasting public spaces.

Creating Parks That Last. The WPA and CCC improved dozens of Dallas parks by constructing trails, picnic shelters, bridges, pavilions, amphitheaters, gardens, retaining walls, and other recreational facilities that remain community landmarks today. Major improvements were made at White Rock Lake, Reverchon Park, Kiest Park, Lake Cliff Park, Tietze Park, Maple Park, Martin Weiss Park, Eighth Street Negro Park, and many other neighborhood parks. Nearly $300,000 in federal funding supported these projects, dramatically expanding the quality and accessibility of Dallas’ park system during one of the nation’s most difficult economic periods.

One of the most significant efforts took place at White Rock Lake, where the Civilian Conservation Corps established a work camp in 1935. Thousands of young men spent the next several years deepening the lake, building roads, picnic areas, trails, shelters, bridges, erosion-control structures, and planting thousands of trees. Many of these rustic stone and timber features still define the park’s character today.

A Lasting Legacy. By the end of the New Deal era in 1940, Dallas’ park system had grown to 94 parks covering more than 5,000 acres. More importantly, these federal investments demonstrated that parks were essential to public infrastructure, providing employment, recreation, conservation, and hope during challenging times. Many of the historic structures built by WPA and CCC workers continue to serve Dallas residents today, standing as enduring reminders of how public investment helped shape one of the city’s greatest community assets.

1935–1936
TEXAS CENTENNIAL ERA

Texas Centennial Era

A World’s Fair That Defined Texas. In 1936, Dallas hosted one of the most ambitious celebrations in Texas history: the Texas Centennial Exposition. Created to commemorate the 100th anniversary of Texas independence from Mexico, the event transformed Fair Park into the center of a statewide celebration that attracted more than six million visitors over 176 days. The exposition not only celebrated Texas history and culture, but also helped boost the economy during the Great Depression and established Dallas as a major city of the American Southwest.

The success of the Centennial was years in the making. Although ideas for a centennial celebration began as early as 1900, official planning accelerated in the 1920s and early 1930s. Dallas secured the honor of hosting the exposition by committing nearly $25 million and building upon the existing State Fair grounds. Under the leadership of Park Board President Jim Dan Sullivan and fellow board members George L. Chestnut, Harry E. Gordon, R. T. Shiels, Martin Weiss, E. J. Railton, and George A. Ripley, Fair Park was transformed into an internationally recognized destination.

Building an Art Deco Landmark. To prepare for the exposition, more than 50 new buildings were constructed at Fair Park, creating what is now the largest and most intact collection of 1930s Art Deco architecture in the world. Designed primarily by architect George Dahl, these structures blended modern design with Texas themes and remain among Dallas’ most treasured landmarks.

Iconic buildings included the Hall of State, a grand native stone building dedicated to Texas history; the Tower Building with its gold-trimmed tower; Centennial Hall; and exhibition spaces showcasing transportation, agriculture, industry, and technology. Today, many of these buildings continue to serve as gathering places, museums, and event venues, preserving the legacy of the Centennial for future generations.

History, Innovation, and Cultural Change. The Texas Centennial Exposition offered visitors a remarkable variety of experiences. Exhibits highlighted Texas history, science, art, and modern technology, while entertainment venues brought music, theater, and cultural performances to life. The Hall of Negro Life, a groundbreaking federally funded pavilion, became the first exhibit at a world’s fair dedicated to recognizing the achievements and contributions of Black Americans. General Electric’s “House of Magic,” Smithsonian dinosaur displays, and Billy Rose’s famous “Casa Mañana” revue demonstrated the excitement and innovation of the era.

Fair Park also hosted history-making events, including the Texas Centennial Olympics in the Cotton Bowl, which featured the first integrated public athletic competition in the American South. Distinguished visitors, including President Franklin D. Roosevelt, Duke Ellington, and Ginger Rogers, further elevated the exposition’s national significance.

The legacy of the Texas Centennial remains visible today in the architecture, museums, monuments, and stories that continue to define Fair Park. More than a celebration of the past, the Centennial helped shape a shared Texas identity and established Fair Park as one of the state’s most important cultural landmarks.

WWII & Post War Era

Parks on the Home Front. World War II reshaped Dallas in ways that extended far beyond its factories and neighborhoods. As the city’s population grew and industry shifted to support the war effort, Dallas parks took on entirely new roles. Spaces once designed for recreation became places of military service, training, housing, and national support.

The most notable transformation at White Rock Lake, where Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC) barracks were converted into a U.S. Army induction center and later a Prisoner of War (POW) camp that housed approximately 300 German soldiers captured from North Africa between 1944 and 1945. Known locally as the “Fritz Ritz” because of the humane conditions, the camp reflected the city’s unexpected role in the global conflict. Elsewhere, portions of the park system were used for military training, while Fair Park supported wartime operations with military repair facilities. Temporary “Victory Huts” also provided housing for military families, recovering servicemen, and nursing students during the war.

Planning for a Growing City. While wartime demands temporarily slowed traditional park development, Dallas leaders were already preparing for the city’s future. The population continued to expand as thousands of workers arrived for defense-related jobs, and local industries—including North American Aviation and the Ford Motor Company—became essential contributors to the Allied war effort.

Recognizing that the city’s rapid growth required a bigger park system, the Dallas Park Board commissioned the nationally recognized planning firm Harland Bartholomew and Associates in 1945 to develop a comprehensive master plan for parks and recreation. Their recommendations helped guide park acquisition, neighborhood recreation, and long-term planning as Dallas entered a new era of suburban expansion.

Following the war, the former White Rock Lake barracks found new life by housing veterans attending Southern Methodist University through the G.I. Bill, demonstrating how park facilities continued serving the community even after military operations had ended.

Leadership and Lasting Growth. The World War II and post-war years were marked by stable leadership that helped guide Dallas through extraordinary change. Longtime Park Board President Ray E. Hubbard, who served from 1942 until 1970, provided continuity during both wartime challenges and the city’s rapid post-war expansion. Other influential board members, including Frederick M. Mayer, Clyde A. Wherry, and Teddy Harris, helped oversee planning efforts that shaped the modern park system.

By the mid-1950s, Dallas Parks had evolved beyond simply providing green space. The system had demonstrated its ability to support national defense, assist returning veterans, and meet the recreational needs of a fast-growing metropolitan community. The planning decisions made during this period laid the groundwork for decades of expansion, ensuring that Dallas parks would continue to serve generations of residents long after the war had ended.

1915–1967
CIVIL RIGHTS ERA

Civil Rights Era

A Fight for Equal Access. For much of the early 20th century, Dallas’ park system reflected the racial segregation that shaped daily life across the city. While parks were intended to provide recreation and gathering places for residents, access was not equal. People of color organized, advocated, and built vibrant community life within the limited spaces available to them, while simultaneously pushing for a more equitable park system. Their perseverance helped transform Dallas parks from symbols of exclusion into places intended for everyone.

Separate Parks, Unequal Resources. In 1915, Dallas established Hall Street Park (now Griggs Park) and Oak Cliff Negro Park (now Eloise Lundy Park), the city’s first parks intended primarily for Black residents. Over the following decades, additional parks, including Moore Park, Wheatley Park, Exline Park, William E. Blair Park, and what is now Juanita Craft Park, served African American neighborhoods. These parks became important community gathering places, hosting baseball games, Juneteenth celebrations, civic meetings, and family events despite receiving far fewer resources than parks serving white residents. At one point, approximately 600 acres of parkland served white residents, while only about 10 acres were designated for communities of people of color.

Though these parks emerged in response to requests from people of color seeking recreational spaces, they also reflected the broader realities of segregation. As historian Michael Phillips observed, “[The parks] were a way to forestall integration,” a reminder that separate facilities were never equal.

Parks Become Places of Progress. Parks and public gathering spaces also became important settings in Dallas’ Civil Rights Movement. At Fair Park, civil rights leader Juanita Craft and the NAACP Youth Council challenged segregation at the State Fair of Texas, organizing peaceful demonstrations that called attention to unequal treatment. Their efforts, along with the work of countless community leaders, helped build momentum for broader civil rights reforms throughout Dallas.

Beginning in the early 1960s, Dallas pursued a negotiated approach to desegregation that became known as the “Dallas Way.” Through collaboration between civic leaders, business leaders, and civil rights advocates, public facilities began opening to all residents. Dallas officially desegregated its parks in June 1963, marking a significant milestone in the city’s history, although achieving true equity would remain an ongoing effort for years to come.

A Lasting Legacy. Today, parks such as Griggs Park, Eloise Lundy Park, Moore Park, and Juanita Craft Park stand as reminders of both the injustices of segregation and the resilience of the communities that shaped them. Their history reflects more than changing policies. It tells the story of neighbors who organized, advocated, celebrated, and persevered. The Civil Rights Era transformed Dallas’ park system, laying the foundation for a future in which public parks could better serve the entire community while honoring the people whose determination made that progress possible.

Modern Era

Beautiful Parks for a Dynamic Dallas. As Dallas has grown into one of America’s fastest-growing metropolitan areas, its park system has evolved alongside it. Since the late twentieth century, the city’s population has expanded dramatically, bringing new neighborhoods, diverse communities, and changing recreational needs.

Today, Dallas Park and Recreation continues to shape a park system that serves more than one million residents while creating destinations that attract visitors from across the region. Population growth across Dallas and the Dallas-Fort Worth metroplex has transformed the role of parks, making them essential places for recreation, community gathering, environmental stewardship, and economic development.

Growing Through Partnerships. One hallmark of the modern era is collaboration. Dallas Park and Recreation has expanded its impact through partnerships with nonprofit organizations and community institutions that help manage and enhance some of the city’s most treasured destinations.

The Dallas Arboretum and Botanical Garden, which opened in 1984 on the shores of White Rock Lake, transformed two historic estates into one of the nation’s premier botanical gardens. Likewise, the Dallas Zoo—owned by the City of Dallas and operated in partnership with the Dallas Zoological Society—has continued to grow into Texas’ oldest and largest zoological park, welcoming more than one million visitors annually. These partnerships allow Dallas Park and Recreation to provide world-class educational, cultural, and conservation experiences while preserving public ownership of these beloved spaces.

Reimagining Urban Green Space. The 21st century ushered in a new vision for parks that reach beyond traditional playgrounds and athletic fields. Downtown parks such as Main Street Garden, Pacific Plaza, Carpenter Park, Harwood Park, and the internationally recognized Klyde Warren Park have transformed underutilized spaces into vibrant public gathering places that reconnect neighborhoods and encourage walkability. Across the city, Dallas continues investing in new neighborhood parks, trail systems, and revitalized community spaces designed to ensure more residents live within a short walk of a park.

Looking to the Future. The story of Dallas parks is still being written. From expanding iconic destinations to developing new neighborhood parks in underserved communities, The Dallas Park and Recreation Department (DPARD) continues adapting to meet the needs of a rapidly changing city. As Dallas grows in size, diversity, and opportunity, its parks remain at the heart of what makes the city a vibrant place to live, connect, and thrive, ensuring that future generations will enjoy a park system as dynamic as Dallas itself.
