Honoring the Legacy of Dallas’ Freedman’s Town

Freedman’s Memorial Cemetery is a 1.7-acre special-use park that preserves one of the most significant African American historic Black sites in Dallas. Established as a city park in 1965, the site honors a cemetery that was originally founded in 1861 as a burial ground for people of color. It served as the final resting place for many residents of Freedman’s Town, a thriving community established by formerly enslaved people following the Civil War.

From 1869 until 1907, the cemetery was an active burial ground for generations of Black Dallas residents. It became an important part of the community’s history, preserving the stories of families who helped build Dallas during Reconstruction and the decades that followed. Today, the memorial park stands as both a place of remembrance and a powerful reminder of the resilience, contributions, and enduring legacy of those early citizens.

A Lost Cemetery Rediscovered

During the mid-1940s, construction of the North Central Expressway dramatically altered the landscape surrounding the cemetery. As development expanded, the historic burial ground was gradually obscured. Gravestones were removed, the cemetery was covered with grass, and the area was converted into a city park. Over time, the cemetery’s location and significance were largely forgotten.

That changed i In the summer of 1990, when renovation work uncovered the long-hidden burial ground. Archaeologists discovered approximately 800 marked graves, along with an estimated 1,200 unmarked burials sites, revealing the extraordinary historical importance of the site. The discovery sparked renewed efforts to preserve the cemetery and properly honor those who had been laid to rest there.

The rediscovery ultimately led to the creation of the Freedman’s Memorial, a space dedicated to remembrance, education, and healing. Today, the site is included on the Uptown Dallas cemetery tour alongside nearby historic cemeteries and has been recognized as a Dallas Landmark since 1992 and as part of the Texas State Historic Marker Program since 1993.

A Memorial Told Through Art

The memorial itself serves as a moving work of public art that tells the story of slavery, emancipation, and hope. In the mid-1990s, the City of Dallas Office of Arts and Culture selected artist David Newton to design a memorial that would respectfully commemorate the lives connected to the cemetery.

Visitors enter through an dramatic archway flanked by two life-size bronze figures by American sculptor and artist David Newton. The “Sentinel,” inspired by the Benin culture of West Africa, stands proudly with a ceremonial machete pointed toward the ground, symbolizing strength and protection. Across from him stands the “Prophetess,” holding a small harp to her chest as a symbol of wisdom, faith, and cultural continuity.

Within the memorial garden, two bronze bas-relief sculptures by David Newton portray the painful realities of enslavement. The “Violated Soul” and the “Struggling Soul” emerge from backgrounds resembling ocean waves, their bound hands and feet recalling the suffering endured during the transatlantic slave trade. Above these sculptures, twelve smaller bronze figures inspired by traditional West African carvings reinforce the connection between African heritage and the lives of those buried here.

At the heart of the memorial stands “Dream of Freedom,” also by Newton, which is a sculpture depicting a newly emancipated couple embracing after slavery. Behind the sculpture is a polished Texas red granite monument engraved with Nia Akimbo’s poem Here, while two original cemetery headstones are preserved within the memorial as tangible links to the site’s past.

Preserving Memory for Future Generations

Throughout the memorial, bronze plaques help tell the story of the cemetery and the people buried there. Additional plaques display poetry written by local schoolchildren who participated in a community poetry contest, creating a meaningful connection between Dallas’ past and its future generations. Granite markers embedded throughout the landscape identify portions of the original cemetery and acknowledge the many unmarked graves that remain beneath the ground.

Today, Freedman’s Memorial Cemetery serves as much more than a historic landmark. It is a place for reflection, education, and remembrance that honors the lives of early Black Dallas residents whose stories might otherwise have been lost to history. By preserving this sacred ground, the memorial ensures that the courage, resilience, and contributions of Freedman’s Town continue to be recognized for generations to come.

As both a historic cemetery and a thoughtfully designed memorial park, Freedman’s Memorial Cemetery stands as one of Dallas’ most meaningful cultural landmarks. Its preservation reminds visitors that understanding the city’s history requires remembering all of those who helped shape it, especially those whose voices were too often overlooked.
