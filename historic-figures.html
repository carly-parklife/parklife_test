Adella K. Turner

1856–1938

A Leader in Dallas’ Women’s Civic Movement. Adella K. Turner was a prominent Dallas social activist whose civic involvement helped expand access to education, literature, and community resources during a transformative period in the city’s history. Born on January 13, 1856, Turner became part of a generation of women who used civic clubs and volunteer organizations to improve public life at a time when women had limited formal political power.

The Standard Club and a Legacy of Community Service. Turner began her activist work through the Standard Club of Dallas, a women’s literary organization founded in 1886. She became the club’s first vice president in 1900. Although literary study was central to the organization, the Standard Club and similar women’s groups became important forces for civic improvement. Their service contributed to the establishment of free local kindergartens, YMCA branches, and the Dallas Public Library.

Turner’s involvement reflected the growing influence of women’s clubs during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. These organizations provided women with opportunities to organize, advocate, raise funds, and address community needs. Through this work, women helped shape public institutions even before they gained the right to vote nationally.

Bringing Library Access to Oak Cliff. One of Turner’s most significant contributions came in 1913, when she helped bring the first free library to Oak Cliff. At a time when access to books and educational resources was far more limited than it is today, a free public library represented an important investment in the community. It provided residents with opportunities for learning, literacy, and personal enrichment regardless of their ability to purchase books.

The library was located in the area now known as Turner Plaza, preserving a connection between Turner’s civic service and the neighborhood she worked to support.

Anderson Bonner

1839–1920

From Enslavement to Freedom. Anderson Bonner’s life is a remarkable story of perseverance, independence, and achievement in the decades following slavery. Born into slavery in Alabama around 1839, Bonner likely came to Texas during the Civil War. When slavery ended in 1865, he began building a new life as a free man in Dallas County.

Bonner married a woman named Eliza, and together they had ten children. Establishing financial security for a large family would have been challenging under any circumstances, but formerly enslaved people faced extraordinary barriers. Discrimination, limited educational opportunities, and restricted access to land and capital made economic advancement difficult for people of color during Reconstruction and the decades that followed.

Building a Remarkable Legacy Through Land Ownership. Despite these obstacles, Bonner became a successful farmer and landowner. The 1870 United States Census listed his net worth at $275, an amount that reflected a measure of economic success at the time. Only a few years later, in April 1874, Bonner purchased 60 acres of land in Dallas.

Unable to read or write, he signed the deed with an “X.” Yet the signature on that document marked the beginning of an extraordinary accumulation of property. Over time, Bonner acquired more than 2,000 acres in what is now North Dallas.

Land ownership represented far more than financial success for formerly enslaved people. It offered independence, stability, and the ability to build wealth for future generations. Bonner’s achievements were particularly significant because they occurred during an era when Black landowners faced systemic discrimination and widespread efforts to restrict their economic and political advancement.

A Legacy Preserved in Dallas. Anderson Bonner died in 1920 at approximately 82 years old. He was buried in White Rock Colored Union Cemetery, now known as White Rock Garden of Memories Cemetery.

In 1976, the City of Dallas officially named Anderson Bonner Park in his honor. The park encompasses more than 44 acres of land that was once part of Bonner’s extensive holdings.

Anita Martinez

1925–2026

Breaking Barriers in Dallas Government. Anita N. Martinez was a pioneering Dallas civic leader whose work in government, culture, and youth development created a lasting legacy. Born on December 8, 1925, Martinez dedicated much of her life to expanding opportunities and representation for Dallas’s Mexican American and Hispanic communities.

In 1969, Martinez made history when she was elected to the Dallas City Council, becoming the first Mexican American elected government official in Dallas. She served on the council until 1973.

Preserving Culture Through Ballet Folklorico. Martinez’s public service extended far beyond elected office. In 1975, she founded the Anita N. Martinez Ballet Folklorico, created to connect young people with Hispanic culture through traditional dance and performance. The organization continues to operate today, extending Martinez’s cultural and educational legacy into new generations.

Creating Opportunities for Dallas Youth. Her commitment to young people was also reflected in the opening of the Anita Martinez Recreation Center in 1976, created as a place for the children of West Dallas to play and participate in community activities. The facility remains part of Dallas’s recreational landscape.

A Legacy of Representation and Service. Throughout her life, Martinez received significant recognition for her community service, including the Zonta Award for Constructive Voluntary Contribution to Community and Civic Affairs and the JCPenney Golden Rule Award for Volunteer Service. She died on April 10, 2026, at the age of 100.

Her legacy can be found in many forms: in the history of Latino representation in Dallas government, in the dancers who have connected with their heritage through Ballet Folklorico, and in the generations of children who have gathered at the recreation center bearing her name.

Edwin J. Kiest

1861–1941

A Publisher Who Helped Shape Dallas. Edwin J. Kiest was a prominent Dallas publisher and civic leader whose influence extended across journalism, broadcasting, public events, and the city’s park system. Born in Illinois in 1861, Kiest moved to Dallas in 1890 and became deeply involved in the growth and development of his adopted city.

In 1896, Kiest purchased the Dallas Daily Times Herald, placing him at the center of the city’s rapidly developing newspaper industry. His interest in communications expanded beyond print journalism: he supported the development of radio station WRR, and in 1927 purchased KRLD, further strengthening his role in the evolution of mass communication in North Texas.

Leadership Beyond the Newsroom. Kiest served as president of the State Fair of Texas from 1908 to 1911 and again from 1920 to 1921, placing him at the center of efforts to promote the city and bring residents together through exhibitions, entertainment, agriculture, and industry.

Kiest also made a significant impact on Dallas’s parks. He served as president of the Dallas City Park Board from 1931 to 1935 and continued as a director until his death in 1941.

The Lasting Legacy of Kiest Park. In 1935, Kiest donated land to the city for the creation of a park honoring his late wife, Elizabeth Patterson Kiest. Kiest Park became a lasting part of Dallas’s park system, and during the 1930s it also benefited from federal relief-era development.

Edwin J. Kiest died in 1941, leaving behind a legacy that touched newspapers and radio, the State Fair of Texas, and the Dallas park system alike.

George Kessler

1862–1923

A Visionary in Landscape Architecture and City Planning. George E. Kessler was an internationally influential landscape architect and city planner whose ideas helped shape Dallas and communities across the United States. Born in Germany in 1862, Kessler immigrated to the United States with his family in 1865 and later returned to Germany to study landscape architecture.

Kessler became known for approaching cities as interconnected systems. Streets, parks, waterways, transportation routes, and neighborhoods could not be planned effectively in isolation. He believed thoughtful planning could improve both the beauty and function of urban environments.

Shaping the Future of Dallas. In 1904, Kessler was hired to design Fair Park in Dallas. In 1909, he was hired to develop a comprehensive city plan for Dallas. The resulting Kessler Plan proposed long-term improvements including measures related to controlling flooding along the Trinity River, improving downtown streets, and creating transportation routes that would help Dallas accommodate future growth.

The plan was ambitious, and many of its ideas were not immediately implemented. Nevertheless, its influence endured, and elements of Kessler’s vision informed later infrastructure and development decisions.

A Lasting Influence Beyond Dallas. Kessler returned to Dallas in 1918 and continued contributing to the city’s planning efforts. His influence was not limited to Dallas — he contributed to plans and projects in communities across the United States, including Cincinnati, Terre Haute, Cleveland, El Paso, Denver, Sherman, and Syracuse.

The Enduring Legacy of the Kessler Plan. George Kessler died in 1923. The lasting significance of the Kessler Plan lies not only in individual projects, but also in the idea that a city’s future could be deliberately planned — that infrastructure, public space, transportation, and the natural environment could work together over generations.

Juanita J. Craft

1902–1985

A Champion for Civil Rights in Dallas. Juanita J. Craft was one of Dallas’s most influential civil rights leaders. Through decades of organizing, protest, voter engagement, and public service, she challenged segregation and worked to expand opportunities for Black Texans.

Born in Round Rock, Texas, in 1902, Craft moved to Dallas by 1925 and became involved with the NAACP in 1935. By 1942, she had become membership chairman of the Dallas NAACP, and in 1946 became a Texas NAACP field organizer, helping establish or organize 182 NAACP branches.

In 1944, Craft became the first Black woman to vote in a Dallas County election.

Leading the Fight Against Segregation. During the 1950s, Craft organized protests challenging segregation at lunch counters, restaurants, theaters, and on public transportation. She also played an important role in efforts to integrate educational institutions, including the University of Texas School of Law and the Dallas Independent School District.

In 1955, Craft led a protest against segregation at the State Fair of Texas, where Black visitors were largely restricted to attending on a designated “Negro Achievement Day.”

From Activist to Public Servant. Craft’s leadership eventually extended into elected office. She served two terms on the Dallas City Council after elections in 1975 and 1979. She was awarded the NAACP Golden Heritage Life Membership Award in 1978 and the Eleanor Roosevelt Humanitarian Award in 1984.

An Enduring Legacy of Equality. Juanita J. Craft died in 1985, but her legacy remains deeply embedded in Dallas history. Parks, recreation facilities, archival collections, and historic sites preserve her connection to the city, including Juanita J. Craft Park and the J.J. Craft House and Museum.

Julien Reverchon

1837–1905

A Pioneer in Texas Botany. Julien Reverchon was a French-born botanist whose exploration and documentation of Texas plant life made him an important figure in the scientific history of the state. Born in France in 1837, at 19 he emigrated to the United States and settled in the Dallas area in 1856.

Reverchon became deeply interested in botany and began studying and collecting the plants of Texas at a time when large portions of the state remained scientifically undocumented.

Exploring the Flora of Texas. In 1879, Reverchon explored West Texas and encountered an unknown plant species that would become associated with his name through the genus Reverchonia. In 1885, he traveled through Southwest Texas, resulting in additional discoveries and contributing valuable information about the diversity of Texas plant life.

Reverchon donated specimens he collected to major scientific institutions, including the United States Department of Agriculture and the Smithsonian Institution.

Sharing Knowledge Through Science and Education. Reverchon taught botany at the Baylor University College of Medicine and Pharmacy in Dallas. By the time of his death in 1905, his farm contained more than 2,600 species and over 20,000 specimens of Texas plants — considered one of the finest collections of its kind.

A Lasting Legacy in Dallas Parks. Reverchon was buried in the cemetery of La Réunion, the historic French colony established near Dallas. His connection to the city also lives on through Reverchon Park, which was named in his honor and remains part of the Dallas park system.

Mildred Louise Dunn

1924–1987

A Career Dedicated to Dallas Parks. Mildred Louise Dunn devoted approximately 30 years of her career to park development in Dallas, contributing to the public spaces and recreational resources that served residents throughout the city. Born in 1924, Dunn belonged to a generation of municipal employees whose sustained work helped Dallas’s park system expand and respond to the needs of a growing population.

Supporting a Growing City. During the mid-twentieth century, Dallas expanded geographically and experienced major demographic and social changes. As neighborhoods developed, the need for parks, playgrounds, recreation centers, athletic facilities, and other public amenities grew as well, and park development employees like Dunn played an important role in helping the city respond.

A Lasting Connection to Lagow Park. Her connection to Dallas parks is preserved through the naming of Lagow Park in her honor. City archival materials identify the site as Lagow, or Mildred Dunn, Park. The park itself has roots in the Works Progress Administration during the New Deal era, and the later association of the park with Mildred Dunn added another layer to its history.

Honoring a Legacy of Public Service. While Dunn died in 1987, her story highlights the many people behind a successful park system — shaped not only by major donors, elected officials, and celebrated designers, but also by career professionals who dedicate decades to planning, developing, and supporting them.

Park Board Members

1905–Present

For 150 years, Dallas parks have grown alongside the city, evolving from a single 10-acre park into an expansive system of parks, recreation centers, trails, and other public spaces. The story of Dallas Park and Recreation is one of vision, community investment and generations of people who helped shape the city’s outdoor spaces.

The Dallas park system began in 1876, when the city acquired 10 acres next to Browder Springs. Known as City Park, it marked the beginning of what would become a major municipal park system.

The Creation of the Park Board. In 1904, Dallas acquired the State Fair Association property that became Fair Park. From 1904 to 1905, the city established the Board of Park Commissioners to oversee park affairs, and Bryan Thomas Barry, mayor of Dallas, appointed the first Dallas Park Board in 1905. The creation of the board provided a dedicated structure for managing parks as the city grew larger, more complex, and more expensive to operate.

Building a Growing Park System. By the end of the New Deal in 1940, Dallas had 94 parks encompassing 5,066 acres. In 1945, the Park Board hired Harland Bartholomew and Associates to develop a new master plan that supported the creation of numerous neighborhood parks and helped guide the system’s postwar growth. By 1951, Dallas had 79 parks covering 5,785 acres; by 1962, the system had grown to 130 parks and nearly 13,337 acres; and by 1977, Dallas had 27 recreation facilities and opened the first six miles of hike-and-bike trails around White Rock Lake.

Planning for the Future. In 1945, the master plan developed by Harland Bartholomew and Associates emphasized neighborhood parks. Decades later, that effort resulted in A Renaissance Plan, completed by Carter & Burgess in 2002 — described in the research as the largest comprehensive urban park study conducted, and unanimously approved by the Dallas Park and Recreation Board and Dallas City Council. Dallas residents subsequently voted overwhelmingly to fund four bond elections during the following decade to help implement its vision.

People Who Shaped Dallas Parks. Behind every park system are the people whose leadership, vision and dedication helped make its growth possible. Several individuals stand out:

  • Laura Yeary Smith (Board Member, 1929–1931) was the first female member appointed to the Dallas Park and Recreation Board, noted for designing the historic picnic and landscape layouts around White Rock Lake.
  • Emil Fretz (Board Member, 1905–1927) was one of the most influential figures in the development of Dallas parks before 1931, shaping park development around the health, happiness and recreation of children, especially in underprivileged areas. He strongly supported the Trinity and Summit Play Park programs and advocated for playground equipment in every park.
  • Julius Schepps (Park Board President, 1970–1971) became Park Board President following Raymond Hubbard’s death and served until his own death in May 1971.
  • Raymond Hubbard (Park Board President, 1943–1970) served longer than nearly any other president in the board’s history, with his lasting impact shaping the city’s parks and recreation system for decades.

Park Board Presidents. From 1905 through 1931, the mayor served as Park Board President, meaning changes in the mayor’s office could also bring changes in leadership at the Park Board. The city manager form of government, established in 1931, brought greater stability to the Park Board and marked a transition in its leadership structure.

Department Directors

1920–Present

Behind every park system are the people whose leadership, vision and dedication helped make its growth possible. Alongside the Park Board, generations of department directors and staff turned the board’s plans into the parks Dallas residents use every day:

  • Foster Jacoby (Department Director, 1920–1937) became the first Superintendent of Parks in 1920 and later served as Director of Park and Recreation. His tenure emphasized active use of Dallas parks and expanded playground and recreation programming. The recreation program he instituted helped Dallas gain recognition in the 1920s for having one of the finer recreation systems in the country.
  • Miss E. Beulah Cauley (Secretary, 1920–1965) began working for the Park Department in 1920 as a secretary and remained with the department until her retirement in 1965. Over nearly five decades, she developed an exceptional knowledge of local parks and the people involved in their development, eventually becoming one of the most influential staff members in the department. Following the 1939 scandals involving park board members, she was appointed Acting Director of Park and Recreation during the interim between Foster Jacoby and L. B. Houston.
  • L. B. Houston (Department Director, 1939–1972) became a key leader of the Park and Recreation Department during a period of significant stress. He is credited with restoring confidence in the department and, alongside Park Board President Raymond Hubbard, helping build what the history describes as a first-rate park system.

150 Years of Looking Ahead. The history of Dallas parks is ultimately a history of growth, planning and people. What began with a few acres at City Park in 1876 developed through new parks, recreation facilities, trails, master plans and major public investments — a foundation that continues to shape how Dallas residents experience parks and recreation today.

Dr. William Worthington “W. W.” Samuell

1878–1937

A Surgeon with a Vision for Dallas. Dr. William Worthington “W. W.” Samuell was a Dallas surgeon, civic leader, and philanthropist whose generosity had a profound impact on the city’s park system. Often referred to as the “father of Dallas parks,” Samuell helped ensure that large areas of land would be preserved for public recreation and enjoyment.

Born in Kentucky in 1878, Samuell moved to Dallas with his family when he was approximately one year old. He attended medical school at Tulane University and continued his professional development in Europe before opening a medical clinic in Dallas in 1914.

Preserving Land for Future Generations. Samuell became deeply interested in the future of Dallas and particularly in the importance of public land. Through major gifts of land and resources, he helped shape the future of the Dallas park system, providing the city with property that could be used for recreation, green space, and community facilities.

The scale of his contributions earned him a lasting place in Dallas history. Parks and properties bearing the Samuell name remain part of the city’s public landscape, including Samuell-Grand Park, Samuell-Elam Park, and Samuell Farm.

A Legacy of Philanthropy. Samuell’s approach to philanthropy reflected a belief that land could become a permanent public asset. His legacy is particularly significant because Dallas continued to grow dramatically after his lifetime — by dedicating property for public purposes, Samuell helped preserve spaces that might otherwise have disappeared.

The Father of Dallas Parks. Dr. W. W. Samuell died in 1937, but his influence continued through the property and resources he left for the benefit of Dallas residents. Today, the Samuell name remains woven into the Dallas park system, demonstrating how one individual’s long-term vision and generosity can shape a city for generations.

William R. Tietze

1859–1933

A Pioneer of Dallas Parks. William Robert Tietze played a foundational role in the development of the Dallas park system. Born in 1859, Tietze was a wholesale florist whose knowledge of plants and landscapes complemented a career that would become closely connected to the growth of public parks in Dallas.

Tietze served as superintendent of the Dallas Park and Recreation Department from 1896 until his death in 1933. When he began his work, Dallas had only one public park. By the end of his career, the system had grown to more than 50 parks.

Growing a Park System for a Growing City. As superintendent, Tietze helped guide Dallas through this important period of park expansion. New properties had to be identified, acquired, developed, and maintained as the city expanded. His background as a florist also connected him to the horticultural dimensions of park development — trees, gardens, lawns, and other plantings helped transform land into parks designed for public enjoyment.

Building the Foundation for Future Growth. His long career also overlapped with major changes in the organization of Dallas parks, including the city charter amendment that created the Dallas Park Board in 1906. Tietze’s legacy is commemorated through Tietze Park, which later benefited from Works Progress Administration improvements during the New Deal era.

A Lasting Legacy of Public Service. William Robert Tietze died in 1933 after approximately 37 years of service to Dallas parks. His legacy can be measured in the extraordinary growth of the system he helped oversee — from one park to more than 50 — and in the idea of a connected municipal park system designed to serve residents across a growing city.
