A Historic Gathering Place Along Turtle Creek

Nestled along Turtle Creek in Dallas’s Oak Lawn neighborhood, Reverchon Park is one of the city’s oldest and most treasured public parks. Originally established in 1914 as Turtle Creek Park, the 46-acre landscape was renamed in 1915 to honor Julien Reverchon, a French botanist, physician, and longtime Dallas resident whose contributions to science and the local community left a lasting legacy.

For more than a century, Reverchon Park has served as a place where Dallas residents come together to enjoy nature, recreation, and community life. With its winding pathways, historic stonework, iconic baseball field, and connection to the Katy Trail, the park continues to be a beloved destination that reflects the evolution of Dallas itself.

A Park Shaped by Nature and History

Reverchon Park’s location played an important role in its early development. The area was historically shaped by the Trinity River and its tributaries, including Turtle Creek, which forms the eastern boundary of the park. The natural water sources, rolling meadows, scenic views, and abundant wildlife attracted early settlers to the area and helped establish the land as an ideal setting for a public park.

The park’s natural landscape continues to reflect the character of North Texas. Mature oak and pecan groves frame the Turtle Creek corridor, creating a shaded woodland setting that connects the park’s recreational spaces with its natural surroundings. Native understory plantings in the flower beds and Iris Garden also provide a botanical connection to the park’s namesake, Julien Reverchon, whose scientific work focused in part on the flora of Texas.

Among the plants associated with Reverchon’s botanical legacy is Reverchon’s hawthorn (*Crataegus reverchonii*), a small ornamental tree or shrub known for its white spring blooms and red pomaceous fruit. The species also provides food and habitat that support local wildlife. Reverchon’s pennyroyal (*Hedeoma reverchonii*), another plant bearing the botanist’s name, is a drought-tolerant member of the mint family. Its lavender-pink tubular flowers thrive along limestone-based woodland edges and prairie transition zones, reflecting the resilient native plant communities of the region.

The original 36 acres that became Reverchon Park were purchased by the City of Dallas in 1915 from the estate of John D. Cole for $31,128. The purchase followed the city’s efforts to improve water quality in Turtle Creek, including the creation of the Turtle Creek Pump Station in 1914.

Over time, the park expanded and evolved into a major recreational destination. In 1946, additional land was added to the southeast corner of the park, bringing it to its current size of approximately 46 acres. Today, the heart of Reverchon Park remains defined by its meandering trails, mature oak and pecan groves, open gathering spaces, native plantings, and peaceful connection to Turtle Creek.

WPA Stonework and a Lasting Landscape Legacy

One of Reverchon Park’s most distinctive features was created during the New Deal era, when the Works Progress Administration (WPA) transformed the park through a series of ambitious improvement projects between 1935 and 1937. As part of a broader effort to enhance Dallas’s public spaces, WPA workers created a remarkable collection of handcrafted stone features that remain central to the park’s identity today.

The centerpiece of this work is the Hillside Terrace, an intricate network of stone staircases, bridges, pathways, overlooks, and seating areas built into the park’s natural bluffs. These elements blend architecture and landscape design, creating scenic viewpoints and unique spaces for visitors to explore.

Additional WPA projects included the floral amphitheater known as the Iris Bowl, a picturesque bridge over Turtle Creek, a fountain constructed as part of the Gill Well project, and other landscaping improvements. These historic features transformed Reverchon Park into one of Dallas’s most beautifully designed recreational spaces.

After periods of decline and reduced maintenance, community efforts helped restore attention and investment to the park. In the late 1990s, the Texas Scottish Rite Hospital for Children, led by J.C. Montgomery, supported a long-term renewal effort. In 2002, hundreds of surgeons visiting Dallas for a convention helped construct the park’s playground, creating a lasting contribution to the community.

A Hub for Baseball, Recreation, and Community Connection

Reverchon Park has long been a destination for sports and recreation. Community baseball games were played at the park as early as 1921, with the current historic ballfield and green bleachers dating back to the 1920s. The grandstand was completed in 1924, and by the late 1930s, the field became the first lighted baseball field in the Dallas Park system.

Over the decades, the field has hosted countless amateur and community baseball games, becoming a cherished part of Dallas sports history. Southern Methodist University’s baseball team also called Reverchon Park home from 1977 until 1980. Today, the historic stadium remains one of the park’s most recognizable features and an important reminder of Dallas’s recreational heritage.

Beyond baseball, Reverchon Park serves as a major access point for the Katy Trail, connecting runners, walkers, and cyclists to one of Dallas’s most popular urban trails. Its location makes it a vital link between neighborhoods, outdoor recreation, and the city’s growing network of green spaces.

In 2016, Reverchon Park was recognized as a Lone Star Legacy Park by the Texas Recreation and Parks Society (TRAPS), honoring its historical significance and enduring importance to the community.

As Dallas continues to grow and change, Reverchon Park remains a place where history, nature, and community come together. Its mature native trees, botanical connections to Julien Reverchon, historic landscapes, WPA craftsmanship, recreational traditions, and dedicated community supporters ensure that this iconic park continues to tell the story of Dallas for generations to come.
